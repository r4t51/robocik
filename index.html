Books

I come back to rooms I can walk through. These three have the lights on.

Invisible Cities

Italo Calvino, 1972

Marco Polo sits with Kublai Khan and describes cities the Khan will never visit. Thin cities. Cities of the dead. A city that is only the memory of a well. Polo is talking about Venice each time, and the Khan begins to hear it.

I keep the book for “Cities & Memory.” A man rides for days and arrives at the city he left, already rewritten by the story he told about it.

The Left Hand of Darkness

Ursula K. Le Guin, 1969

Genly Ai is a visitor on Gethen, a winter planet where people are neither men nor women for most of the month. He mistrusts Estraven, the one person willing to cross the Gobrin ice with him.

The book is that crossing. A tent, a sledge, two people who learn how to speak to each other. I open it when I want a story that treats trust as work.

The Master and Margarita

Mikhail Bulgakov, 1967

The devil walks into Moscow with a black cat and a small crew. Apartments empty. A writer burns his novel. Margarita agrees to host the devil’s ball so she can ask for the man she loves.

The Moscow chapters stay funny in a way that still bites, and the ball runs long, hot, and cruel. I read those pages for a city that keeps its jokes while something impossible sits at the table.

Films

I watch films that leave a room in frame long enough to show who left it.

Stalker

Andrei Tarkovsky, 1979

A guide, a writer, and a professor enter the Zone. The path is wet grass, shallow water, and a tunnel they crawl. The camera stays close to the ground and refuses to hurry.

I put it on when I have the evening. The film wants the walk.

Chungking Express

Wong Kar-wai, 1994

Two stories share a snack bar in Hong Kong. Cop 223 buys tins of pineapple dated to expire. Cop 663 talks to his soap and his towel. Faye slips into his flat and changes the labels while “California Dreamin’” plays on repeat.

The second story starts at the counter where the first one ended. I replay the bar for the way one small room holds two heartbreaks.

The Spirit of the Beehive

Víctor Erice, 1973

Ana sees Frankenstein in a traveling cinema and decides the monster lives in an abandoned sheepfold outside the village. Her father keeps bees. Her mother writes letters. The camera stays with Ana’s face while the adults move through their own quiet trouble.

I come back for the classroom and the well. Both are shot as if the child is the only person who can see the frame.

Games

These three want a notebook. You carry the last session into the next one.

Outer Wilds

Mobius Digital, 2019

You wake beside a campfire on Timber Hearth, fly a wooden ship, and read the ruins of a people who died before you arrived. The sun explodes. You wake at the campfire again. The ship computer still holds what you translated.

The ending comes when you know where to stand. I play it in long sittings. A short visit only teaches the controls.

Return of the Obra Dinn

Lucas Pope, 2018

An insurance inspector boards a ship that came home with no living crew. A pocket watch shows the moment each person died. You match faces to names and write the cause in a book. The pictures are stark. The decks are a maze.

A name locks, and the book accepts it. I stay with the chapter where the crabs arrive. The picture gives you the answer if you look at who holds the lantern.

Disco Elysium

ZA/UM, 2019

You play a detective in Martinaise who drank his own name out of his head. The skills talk. Inland Empire whispers. Authority shouts. The case is a hanging behind a hostel, and the city is the larger case.

I stay for the church on the coast and the talk with the phasmid. Both treat a ruined man as someone who can still listen.
